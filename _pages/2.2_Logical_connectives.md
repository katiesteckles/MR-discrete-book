The main **logical connectives** used in propositional logic are essentially slightly more precisely defined equivalents of the English words 'and', 'or' and 'not'. We can combine existing propositions using these connectives to produce new ones. These methods were introduced by the English mathematician George Boole in 1854 in his book **The Laws of Thought**.


```{admonition} Definition 2.3 - Compound Proposition
:label: compound-proposition-definition

A **compound proposition** is one which has been formed from two or more atomic propositions using logical connectives.

The truth value of a compound proposition depends upon the truth values of its component atomic propositions.

```

All non-atomic propositions are compound propositions.

For example: ‘I am a student at university, and I am studying for a maths degree’ is a compound proposition. It is constructed from two atomic propositions: ‘I am a student at university’ and ‘I am studying for a maths degree’.

The word 'and' which, connects the two propositions, is a **logical connective**. All compound propositions contain at least one logical connective.

There are three basic logical connectives: $\texttt{AND}$, $\texttt{OR}$ and $\texttt{NOT}$.

## $\texttt{AND}$

```{admonition} Definition 2.4 - The connective \texttt{AND}$ (conjunction)
:label: conjunction-definition

Let $p$ and $q$ be propositions. The **conjunction** of $p$ and $q$, created using the connective $\texttt{AND}$, is denoted $p \land q$ (pronounced 'p and q').

The truth table for $p \land q$ is given by
```

## $\texttt{NOT}$

```{admonition} Definition 2.6 - The connective $\texttt{NOT}$ (negation)
:label: conjunction-definition

Let $p$ be a proposition. The **negation** of $p$, created using the connective $\texttt{NOT}$, is denoted $\not p$ (pronounced 'not p'), and is a proposition that has the opposite truth value to that of the original proposition.

The truth table for $\not p$ is given by

<table style="width:50%">
   <tr><th align="center">p</th><th align="center">&not; p</th></tr>
   <tr>
      <td align="center">T</td>
      <td align="center">F</td>
   </tr>
   <tr>
      <td align="center">F</td>
      <td align="center">T</td>
   </tr>
</table>

We say that if a proposition is true then its negation is false, and if the proposition is false then its negation is true.

```

For example, let p represent the proposition "There is a bug in the program". Then, $\not p$ stands for the proposition "It is not the case that there is a bug in the program", or "There is not a bug in the program".

## Combining multiple connectives

We can produce more complex statements using multiple connectives. Brackets can be used to make clear the order in which the propositions are to be combined.

For example, the statement if $p$ represents the statement 'my computer is broken', $q$ represents 'ghosts are real' and $r$ represents 'ghosts are out to get me', we can write the statement 'Either my computer is broken, or ghosts are real and they're out to get me' as $p \lor (q \land r)$.

# Implication connectives

In addition to the three basic logical connectives $\texttt{AND}$, $\texttt{OR}$ and $\texttt{NOT}$, there are two
implication connectives: **conditional** and **biconditional**. These correspond to the English language constructions 'if... then' and 'if and only if'.

## Conditionals

```{admonition} Definition 2.6 - The conditional
:label: conditional-definition

Let $p$ and $q$ be propositions. The **conditional**, stated 'if $p$ then $q$', is denoted $p \Rightarrow q$ (or sometimes $p \rightarrow q$). The conditional is true when the values of $p$ and $q$ are consistent with this relationship - that whenever $p$ is true, $q$ is true.

The truth table for $p \Rightarrow q$ is given by

<table style="width:50%">
   <tr><th align="center">p</th><th align="center">q</th><th align="center">p &8658; q</th></tr>
   <tr>
      <td align="center">T</td>
      <td align="center">T</td>
      <td align="center">T</td>
   </tr>
   <tr>
      <td align="center">T</td>
      <td align="center">F</td>
      <td align="center">F</td>
   </tr>
      <tr>
      <td align="center">F</td>
      <td align="center">T</td>
      <td align="center">T</td>
   </tr>
    <tr>
      <td align="center">F</td>
      <td align="center">F</td>
      <td align="center">T</td>
   </tr>
</table>

Note that if $p$ is false, this one-way conditional is true regardless of the truth value of $q$.

In the statement $p \Rightarrow q$, $p$ is sometimes called the **hypothesis** (or **antecedent**, or **premise**)
and $q$ is called the **conclusion** (or **consequence**).

```

For example, let $p$ and $q$ represent the following propositions: $p$ = 'The demand rises' and $q$ = 'The companies expand'. Then $p \Rightarrow q$ can be stated as ‘If demand rises, then companies expand’.

```{admonition} Note
:class: important

The following phrases are all ways to state $p \Rightarrow q$:

- if $p$, then $q$
- $p$ implies $q$
- $q$ follows from $p$
- not $p$ unless $q$
- $q$ if $p$
- whenever $p$, $q$; $q$ whenever $p$
- $p$ is sufficient (or a sufficient condition) for $q$
- $p$ only if $q$
- $q$ is necessary (or a necessary condition) for $p$
```

```{admonition} Definition 2.9 - The Biconditional
:label: biconditional-definition

The truth table for $p \Leftrightarrow q$ is given by

<table style="width:50%">
```

```{admonition} Note
:class: important

- $p$ if and only if $q$
- $p$ is equivalent to $q$
- $p$ implies $q$ and $q$ implies $p$
```

```{admonition} Theorem 2.1 - Equivalence of Conditionals
:label: equivalence-of-conditionals-theorem
:class: attention

- An implication and its contrapositive are logically equivalent.
- The converse and inverse of an implication are logically equivalent, and the inverse is the contrapositive of the converse.
- An implication is not logically equivalent to its converse (and thus not to its inverse).

```

We will prove the first statement by comparing the truth tables of p  q and  q  p.
