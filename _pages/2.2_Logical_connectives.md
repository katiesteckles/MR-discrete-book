# Logical Connectives


The main **logical connectives** used in propositional logic are essentially slightly more precisely defined equivalents of the English words 'and', 'or' and 'not'. We can combine existing propositions using these connectives to produce new ones. These methods were introduced by the English mathematician George Boole in 1854 in his book **The Laws of Thought**.


```{admonition} Definition 2.3 - Compound Proposition
:label: compound-proposition-definition

A **compound proposition** is one which has been formed from two or more atomic propositions using logical connectives.

The truth value of a compound proposition depends upon the truth values of its component atomic propositions.

```

All non-atomic propositions are compound propositions.

For example: ‘I am a student at university, and I am studying for a maths degree’ is a compound proposition. It is constructed from two atomic propositions: ‘I am a student at university’ and ‘I am studying for a maths degree’.

The word 'and' which, connects the two propositions, is a **logical connective**. All compound propositions contain at least one logical connective.

There are three basic logical connectives: $\texttt{AND}$, $\texttt{OR}$ and $\texttt{NOT}$.

## $\texttt{AND}$

```{admonition} Definition 2.4 - The connective $\texttt{AND}$ (conjunction)
:label: conjunction-definition

Let $p$ and $q$ be propositions. The **conjunction** of $p$ and $q$, created using the connective $\texttt{AND}$, is denoted $p \land q$ (pronounced 'p and q').

The truth table for $p \land q$ is given by

   </tr>
   <tr>

   </tr>
      <tr>

We say that the compound proposition consisting of two simple propositions $p$ and $q$, joined together by the connective $\texttt{AND}$, is true only when both component propositions are true and is false otherwise.
```

For example, consider the compound proposition: "I have a good job AND I am happy." This is true only if the simple proposition components, "I have a good job" and "I am happy" are both true.

```{admonition} Definition 2.5 - The connective $\texttt{OR}$ (disjunction)
:label: disjunction-definition

Let $p$ and $q$ be propositions. The **disjunction** of $p$ and $q$, created using the connective $\texttt{OR}$, is denoted $p \lor q$ (pronounced 'p or q').

   </tr>

   </tr>

We say that the compound proposition consisting of two simple propositions $p$ and $q$, joined together by the connective $\texttt{OR}$, is false only when both component propositions are false and is true otherwise. We can also say that the disjunction of two propositions is true if at least one of the propositions is true.

```

For example, let $p$ and $q$ represent the propositions "There is a bug in the program" and "There is an error in the input data". Then $p \lor q$ translates as "There is a bug in the program or there is an error in the input data."

## $\texttt{NOT}$

```{admonition} Definition 2.6 - The connective $\texttt{NOT}$ (negation)
:label: negation-definition

Let $p$ be a proposition. The **negation** of $p$, created using the connective $\texttt{NOT}$, is denoted $\neg p$ (pronounced 'not p'), and is a proposition that has the opposite truth value to that of the original proposition.

The truth table for $\neg p$ is given by

<table style="width:50%">
   <tr><th align="center">p</th><th align="center">&not; p</th></tr>
   <tr>
      <td align="center">T</td>
      <td align="center">F</td>
   </tr>
   <tr>
      <td align="center">F</td>
      <td align="center">T</td>
   </tr>
</table>

We say that if a proposition is true then its negation is false, and if the proposition is false then its negation is true.

```

For example, let p represent the proposition "There is a bug in the program". Then, $\neg p$ stands for the proposition "It is not the case that there is a bug in the program", or "There is not a bug in the program".

```{admonition} Note
:class: important

Notation for logical operators is not standardized.

- While $p \land q$ is the most common notation for the connective $\texttt{AND}$, you sometimes also see $p \& q$, $p \cdot q$ and $pq$
- For $p \lor q$, sometimes $p + q$ is used for the connective $\texttt{OR}$
- Although $\neg p$ is standard for the negation of $p$, you might see $\sim p$, $−p$, $p'$, $Np$, and $!p$.
```

## Combining multiple connectives

We can produce more complex statements using multiple connectives. Brackets can be used to make clear the order in which the propositions are to be combined.

For example, the statement if $p$ represents the statement 'my computer is broken', $q$ represents 'ghosts are real' and $r$ represents 'ghosts are out to get me', we can write the statement 'Either my computer is broken, or ghosts are real and they're out to get me' as $p \lor (q \land r)$.

```{admonition} Note
:class: important

We call $\neg$ a **unary connective**, because it takes a single proposition and returns its negation. All the other connectives we will consider are **binary connectives**, as they take two propositions and combine them to give a single truth value.

The binary connectives $\land$ and $\lor$ are **symmetric**, in that the order of the two propositions being joined by the connective does not matter - the truth value of $p \land q$ is the same as the truth value of $q \land p$. We will later see some examples of non-symmetric binary logical connectives.

```

For more complex compound propositions, there are some precedence rules for logical connectives.

- As with arithmetic, brackets around expressions need to be considered first.
- The connective $\neg$ then has the highest precedence. Therefore, $\neg p \lor q$ can
be understood as $(\neg p)\lor q$, and not $\neg (p\lor q)$
- In the case of binary connectives, $\land$ takes precedence over $\lor$ - that is, $p \land q \lor r$ should be read as $(p \land q) \lor r$, not $p \land (q \lor r)$.

If the precedence rules are not sufficient to remove all ambiguities, then brackets must be
used.

## Logic circuits

Computer logic circuits use **logic gates** which implement some of these logical operations. Digital circuits use signals that are each either $0$ (off) or $1$ (on), represented by high or low voltage in the wire. The gates receive one or more input signals, and produce output signals according to specific rules.
